DUI fines and immigration removal criteria in Korea

Category Immigration · CriminalReading time approx. 7 min

Contents

A foreign national fined for DUI may face an immigration violation review based on the fine and previous criminal penalties. The review assesses the risk posed by the offence and grounds for continued residence. The fine amount alone does not determine whether a departure or deportation order will follow.

DUI sentencing ranges and aggravating factors

A DUI case may turn on whether driving occurred, the blood alcohol concentration and testing process, any accident, and previous penalties. The statutory ranges for an ordinary DUI offence are set out below.

Blood alcohol concentrationStatutory penalty for ordinary DUI
0.03% to below 0.08%Up to one year of imprisonment or a fine of up to KRW 5 million
0.08% to below 0.2%One to two years of imprisonment or a fine of KRW 5–10 million
0.2% or aboveTwo to five years of imprisonment or a fine of KRW 10–20 million

The table covers ordinary DUI involving motor vehicles and other covered vehicles, excluding personal mobility devices. Separate provisions may apply to repeat offences, refusal of or interference with testing, and accidents causing injury or death.

Removal criteria based on fines and repeat offences

The review criteria for foreign nationals with final criminal fines include a fine of at least KRW 3 million, cumulative fines of at least KRW 5 million within five years, or a fine or more severe punishment on at least two occasions within two years or three occasions within five years. Meeting any one criterion may bring a person within the category ordinarily subject to removal.

These internal guidelines identify cases potentially warranting removal. They do not assign a departure order to a KRW 3 million fine and deportation to a KRW 5 million fine. Even when a threshold is met, the lawfulness of the decision depends on an assessment of the individual circumstances.

Small individual fines can still meet the repeat-offence criteria, while a first offence can meet the monetary threshold. Any separate criteria for the particular type of offence must also be considered.

These amounts and frequencies concern criminal penalties imposed by courts. Immigration penalties and administrative fines are not added to criminal fines.

Sentencing factors and grounds for residence

Restitution, settlement and remorse may support mitigation in the criminal case. The later immigration review applies removal criteria to the final sentence and considers grounds for continued residence or exemption from an entry ban.

The immigration review examines the nature and repetition of the offence, risks to public safety and social order, and the need to remain in Korea. A person who receives a fine or a suspended sentence may still be ordered to leave following that review.

Reoffending risk and family or business ties

Family ties, care of minor children, an established life in Korea, medical needs and business operations may support the need to remain and demonstrate the harm caused by departure. Living with family or running a business for many years does not itself secure permission to stay; the authority considers whether those circumstances support an exception to removal.

DUI cases raise concerns about road safety and reoffending. A further offence after leniency or a pledge to comply with the law can make it harder to obtain permission to remain on family or financial grounds.

Lawyeon identifies the circumstances already assessed in earlier criminal and immigration proceedings and presents submissions and evidence showing why current care responsibilities or business duties support continued residence.

Challenges to removal and entry-ban relief

An objection to a deportation order under the Immigration Control Act must be filed within seven days of receipt. That procedure does not apply to a departure order. A revocation lawsuit addresses grounds for cancelling the decision, while an application for suspension addresses the requirements for interim relief pending judgment.

Filing suit does not automatically suspend the obligation to leave or enforcement. A person subject to a departure order must observe its deadline even if time remains under the previous permission to stay.

An exemption from or lifting of an entry ban leaves the obligation to depart intact if the departure order remains in force. After departure, re-entry requires assessment of the remaining ban and grounds for relief, followed by compliance with the visa and admission requirements for the intended purpose.
