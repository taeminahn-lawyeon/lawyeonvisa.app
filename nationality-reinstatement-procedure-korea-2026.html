Korean nationality reinstatement and retaining foreign citizenship

Category NationalityReading time approx. 4 min

Contents

For former Korean citizens planning to settle in Korea, eligibility to retain foreign citizenship can determine when to apply for nationality reinstatement. As a general rule, a person who acquires Korean nationality through reinstatement must renounce foreign nationality within one year of acquisition. Eligible permanent returnees aged 65 or over, among other specified applicants, may instead pledge not to exercise foreign nationality in Korea.

Nationality reinstatement is available before age 65. For someone who wishes to retain both nationalities and does not qualify for another exception to the renunciation requirement, one option is to apply once eligible under the rules for older permanent returnees. Residence in Korea can begin before that application.

Returning before 65 with F-4 status

Consider a 63-year-old foreign citizen who wants to return to Korea to live with family while retaining foreign citizenship. If eligible for F-4 status, the person can obtain that status, complete domestic residence registration and settle in Korea before applying for nationality reinstatement at age 65. The age requirement for this dual-nationality route is assessed at the time of the reinstatement application. Registering a domestic residence before turning 65 does not itself disqualify the applicant.

Other exceptions to the renunciation requirement cover, for example, people adopted by foreigners before reaching adulthood who acquired foreign nationality and continued to live abroad, as well as people recognized as meeting the requirements for special contributions or outstanding talent. Eligible applicants may qualify to make the non-exercise pledge before age 65 and need not defer their application solely to use the route for older returnees.

Permanent settlement and the review in Korea

The exception for older returnees applies to people returning to settle permanently in Korea. Domestic residence registration provides a basis for recognizing that intention. Applicants must maintain both their domestic residence registration and their intention to settle throughout the review.

A plan to continue living abroad and submit the application during a brief visit to Korea will therefore have difficulty meeting this requirement. Temporary travel abroad for personal reasons is permitted, but the review is put on hold while the applicant is outside Korea. Applicants who need to travel temporarily should notify the immigration office of their departure and return.

A person living abroad may also submit a general nationality reinstatement application through the Korean diplomatic mission responsible for their address. An overseas application, however, does not establish eligibility for the renunciation exception based on permanent return at age 65 or over. Unless another exception applies, such as the applicable provisions for overseas adoptees, a person who obtains Korean nationality through an overseas application remains subject to the foreign-nationality renunciation requirement.

Following approval, Korean nationality is generally acquired when the applicant takes the oath of allegiance and receives the nationality reinstatement certificate. An eligible applicant must make the non-exercise pledge within one year of that acquisition. The pledge replaces the obligation to renounce foreign nationality under Korean law. The person is then treated as a Korean national in Korea and uses a Korean passport to enter and leave the country.

Reporting nationality loss and resolving identity discrepancies

Where a name or date of birth in foreign documents differs from Korean records, the reinstatement process requires evidence linking the former Korean identity to the current foreign identity. A Korean citizen who voluntarily acquires foreign citizenship loses Korean nationality on the date of acquisition. If the loss has not yet been reported, the same identity issue may arise when recording it in the Korean family relations register.

Following a surname change on marriage or a lawful name change abroad, both names may accurately reflect the person’s identity at different times. A marriage certificate or name-change order connecting the former and current names can be matched against Korean family records and the current passport to establish identity. The difference alone does not require the person to change their current foreign name back to the name previously used in Korea.

A discrepancy in dates of birth calls for evidence of the actual birth date and how each record was created. If the birth date in the Korean family relations register is incorrect and evidence supports the correct date, an application can be made to the family court for permission to correct the Korean record. Where a foreign record requires correction, the relevant country’s procedures apply.

This matters particularly when different birth years mean one record places the applicant at 65 or over while the other places them below that age. Establishing that both records refer to the same person does not, by itself, resolve eligibility for the age-based exception. Evidence must support the birth date used to assess the applicant’s age, with incorrect records corrected where necessary.
