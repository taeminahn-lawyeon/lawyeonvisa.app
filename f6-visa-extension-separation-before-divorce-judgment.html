F-6 Visa Extension During Separation: Is a Final Divorce Required?

Law Firm Lawyeon2026.09.18

Contents

A foreign national separated from a Korean spouse may face two different objections when seeking an F-6 extension. A marriage that remains legally registered may no longer support residence as a spouse in an ongoing marital relationship. At the same time, immigration authorities may refuse to consider residence based on marital breakdown because no divorce has been finalized. The issue is which facts each residence category actually requires.

In its first-instance judgment of 13 August 2026, case 2025GuDan53041, the Seoul Administrative Court found that the applicant no longer met the F-6-1 requirements but did meet the requirements associated with marital breakdown under F-6-3. It cancelled the extension refusal. The absence of a final divorce did not, by itself, exclude that basis for residence.

A registered marriage does not settle F-6-1 eligibility

The couple had lived apart for approximately thirteen years when the refusal was issued. The court considered whether cohabitation was likely to resume, their contact and shared assets, the Korean spouse’s withdrawal of sponsorship, and her stated intention to divorce. Payments made by the applicant were acknowledged, but were not considered to exceed the level of child support.

The applicant also relied on principles concerning the genuineness of a marriage at its inception. The court distinguished that family-law inquiry from the review of an immigration refusal. Extension eligibility is assessed at the time of the administrative decision. Evidence that the marriage was genuine when it began does not establish that the required marital relationship continued many years later.

Where current marital life is in issue, a marriage certificate and the original history of the relationship may therefore be insufficient. If the relationship has broken down, the analysis should also address the residence requirements applicable to that breakdown, rather than depending entirely on an assertion that the marriage continues.

The central issue is primary responsibility for the breakdown

F-6-3 addresses, among other situations, a person who was residing in Korea while married to a Korean national and can no longer maintain a normal marital relationship for reasons not attributable to that person. Applying Supreme Court principles, the court did not interpret this as requiring the foreign spouse to be entirely free from fault. It examined who bore primary responsibility for the breakdown.

The Korean spouse’s unilateral move with the child was significant. The couple had established their home in Seoul, where the applicant worked, and the evidence did not establish meaningful agreement on the relocation. The court also considered his continued proposals to live together, her refusal coupled with housing demands that did not reflect their financial circumstances, and his sustained child-support payments.

The same payment records served different evidential purposes. They did not establish that normal married life continued, but they were relevant to his ongoing support of the child and the assessment of responsibility. Presenting the records requires an explanation of the proposition they support, rather than treating any financial contact as proof that the marriage remains intact.

The manual’s reference to divorce did not add a final-divorce requirement

Immigration authorities argued that the manual described marital breakdown by reference to death, disappearance and divorce, and that the applicant could not qualify without a legal divorce. The court focused on the Enforcement Decree: it addresses an inability to maintain a normal marital relationship without expressly requiring a completed divorce.

The manual did not state that it narrowed the Decree’s scope. Its supporting-document list also included a divorce complaint, not only a divorce judgment. The court regarded this as consistent with assessing a breakdown before divorce is complete. A short heading in the manual could not establish a restriction absent from the governing provision.

Implications for parallel divorce and immigration proceedings

The judgment supports addressing residence eligibility before the divorce process ends. Where the relationship has broken down and the Korean spouse bears primary responsibility, those circumstances can be raised in an extension review independently of whether the divorce is final. Equally, the continued legal registration of a marriage should not be treated as sufficient proof of F-6-1 eligibility.

The relevant evidence needs to explain how separation began, attempts to resume shared life, the other spouse’s response, and the history of support. If a family-court pleading and an immigration interview describe the same events differently, the reason for the difference matters. Marriage validity, responsibility for divorce and immigration eligibility ask different legal questions; a favorable proposition in one procedure does not automatically answer another.

This is an analysis of a first-instance judgment on particular facts. It does not establish that every separated applicant qualifies for F-6-3, or remove scrutiny of the applicant’s conduct and actual married life. Its practical significance is that the absence of a final divorce should not replace an assessment of marital breakdown and primary responsibility.
