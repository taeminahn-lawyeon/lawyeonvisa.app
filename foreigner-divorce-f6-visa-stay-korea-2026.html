F-6 residence requirements after divorce from a Korean spouse

Updated 2026.09.18Category Residence & FamilyReading time approx. 7 min

Contents

A foreign national divorced from a Korean spouse may remain in Korea under F-6-2 status to raise a minor child. Where the Korean spouse bears primary responsibility for the marriage breakdown, the foreign spouse may apply to remain under F-6-3. A person who qualifies for neither may apply for F-1-6 status to divide property or settle family matters. The relevant criteria address actual childcare, the cause of the marriage breakdown, and family matters that require the applicant’s presence in Korea.

F-6 status after divorce

Divorce ends the basis for F-6-1 status as the spouse of a Korean national. Applicants who meet the childcare or marriage-breakdown requirements may continue to reside under F-6 status.

SubcategoryWho it coversAfter divorce
F-6-1 Spouse of a nationalA person validly married to a Korean national and continuing married lifeBasis extinguished when the marriage ends
F-6-2 Child-rearingA parent raising, or intending to raise, in Korea a minor child born of the marriage (including de facto marriage) with a nationalStay continues if child-rearing is recognized
F-6-3 Marriage dissolutionA person unable to maintain the marriage due to the spouse's death or disappearance, or other causes not attributable to themStay continues if lack of fault is substantiated

F-6-3 status is available through an application in Korea, rather than a visa issued by an overseas mission. A person who already holds F-6-1 status generally applies for an extension on childcare or marriage-breakdown grounds.

Childcare and visitation under F-6-2

A person raising, or intending to raise, in Korea a minor child born of the marriage with a national (including de facto marriage) may stay under child-rearing status (F-6-2). The reviewing officer verifies the applicant’s actual care of the child and conducts a field investigation if necessary.

A parent who does not directly raise the child may still qualify by showing visitation rights and continuing contact. Where visitation has been restricted or excluded by a family court decision, or there is no contact with the child, stay is not permitted.

F-6-2 eligibility depends on the child’s need for care, without attributing fault for the marriage breakdown. Applicants with minor children should therefore first assess their eligibility for F-6-2. Each extension may be granted for up to three years (as of 2026).

Marriage breakdown and spousal fault under F-6-3

A person without minor children may remain under F-6-3 where the marriage can no longer continue because the spouse has died or disappeared, or for other reasons not attributable to the applicant. This includes cases in which the Korean spouse bears primary responsibility for the breakdown, such as desertion or violence.

The claim that the applicant was not responsible for the breakdown must be supported by evidence. Court records, investigative or medical records, and third-party confirmations can substantiate the account of how the marriage ended. Evidence of violence or desertion can become harder to obtain over time, so preparation needs to begin before divorce proceedings.

An applicant at fault for the breakdown who supports the Korean spouse’s parents or family may provide evidence of that support and receive permission to stay for up to one year (as of 2026).

F-1-6 status and residence during divorce proceedings

An applicant who qualifies for neither F-6-2 nor F-6-3 may remain under F-1-6 where their presence in Korea is necessary to divide property or settle family matters. Permission is granted for up to six months at a time, with a maximum of one year from the change of status. If related litigation, such as a claim for the return of a deposit, continues beyond that year, the applicant may remain under G-1 status until the proceedings end (as of 2026).

For applicants who are separated or in divorce proceedings, F-6-1 extension review examines whether a normal marital relationship exists at the time of the decision. If the marriage has broken down and the Korean spouse bears primary responsibility, eligibility under F-6-3 may be considered before the divorce is final. See our analysis of the judgment on F-6 residence before a final divorce.

Evidence of fault after divorce by agreement

Documents recording a divorce by agreement often do not establish responsibility for the marriage breakdown. Where a couple without minor children divorced by agreement following the Korean spouse’s desertion, proof of the divorce needs to be accompanied by evidence of when the spouse left and how that departure brought married life to an end.

The duration of the marriage, the couple’s actual life together, and the events leading to the breakdown are examined to establish the Korean spouse’s primary responsibility. Evidence of the applicant’s life in Korea and need to remain is also relevant, but the documents must substantiate fault as the basis of the application under F-6-3.

Expiry dates and applications to extend or change status

If the current period of stay will expire before the divorce is final, the appropriate status is assessed on the basis of the couple’s actual married life, childcare, and responsibility for the breakdown. The timetable for an extension or change of status must account for the expiry date and when evidence of the relevant requirements will be available.

Frequently asked questions

Does divorce always mean leaving Korea?
No. A person raising minor children may stay under child-rearing status (F-6-2); a person not at fault for the breakdown under dissolution status (F-6-3); and otherwise, where property division or family matters require it, under family-affairs status (F-1-6).
Can I extend my visa while divorce litigation is pending?
An extension may be considered during divorce proceedings where the relevant residence requirements are met. The continued legal registration of a marriage does not by itself establish F-6-1 eligibility. Where the marriage has broken down and the Korean spouse bears primary responsibility, F-6-3 eligibility may be considered before divorce is final.
