Korea F-4 Visa: DUI, Departure Orders and Humanitarian Grounds

The Review and Decision Criteria for Foreign Nationals with Final Criminal Fines provide for consideration of permission to remain where humanitarian circumstances warrant it, even if the criteria for removal are met. For an overseas Korean living in Korea on an F-4 visa and supporting a family here, the assessment of family life in Korea, alongside the criminal sentence, can provide grounds for challenging a departure order.

Even where the sentence meets the removal criteria, the assessment of family life in Korea can therefore be challenged. The arguments in legal submissions and appeals depend on whether relevant humanitarian circumstances were overlooked or whether those circumstances were acknowledged but the seriousness of the offence was considered to require departure.

Humanitarian grounds without a Korean spouse

The Guidelines on the Handling of Entry Restrictions and Related Matters recognize humanitarian circumstances extending beyond spouses and lineal relatives of Korean nationals or permanent residents. They also include foreign nationals of Korean descent who have established their lives in Korea, and people raising minor children with an established home and livelihood here. The fact that a spouse and children are all foreign nationals is not, by itself, a basis for excluding family life in Korea from humanitarian consideration.

Living together as a family in Korea, sending children to school here, and earning a livelihood through employment or business all support the existence of an established life in Korea. Explaining the gaps in childcare or loss of family income that a parent’s departure would cause can also demonstrate why continued residence or re-entry is needed. A family member does not have to develop a serious illness before humanitarian grounds can be raised.

How ties to Korea were assessed in DUI departure-order cases

Long residence alone has not been enough to overturn a departure order. In one case, an overseas Korean with F-4 status who had lived in Korea for about eight years received a KRW 8 million fine for driving under the influence (DUI). The person relied on having no previous conviction and having worked in Korea. However, the assessment also took into account the high blood alcohol concentration, the absence of an established family in Korea, and the lack of evidence of long-term employment at a particular workplace. The departure order was upheld.

By contrast, a departure order was overturned in a case involving an E-9 non-professional employment visa holder fined KRW 5 million for DUI. The original review decision stated that there were no humanitarian grounds. On administrative appeal, however, the spouse’s employment in Korea, the child’s birth in Korea, and the person’s long-term employment were confirmed, establishing that the family had built its life here. The absence of other criminal or regulatory violations and of accident-related injury or property damage was also considered.

Challenging a finding of “no humanitarian grounds”

Where a removal decision rests on a finding that there are no humanitarian grounds, a challenge addresses the specific facts that contradict that finding. If the spouse’s employment, childcare arrangements, and the person’s livelihood in Korea are established but were not taken into account, identifying how those facts fall within the grounds recognized by the guidelines can expose an omission or error in the assessment. If the authority did not consider an exception permitting continued residence because the removal criteria were met, the issue is whether it exercised its discretion by applying the review criteria to the individual circumstances.

Family records may establish that a person has children, but a claim that the spouse cannot readily care for them alone needs support from the actual division of childcare responsibilities and the spouse’s working arrangements or health. Similarly, a parent’s medical certificate establishes an illness; explaining the need to remain in Korea also requires evidence of the help currently needed and the person’s role in providing it.

Where the authority acknowledges family life but considers departure necessary because of repeated offending, submitting more of the same family records does little to address the reason for the decision. Reoffending after previously receiving permission to remain is particularly adverse because the person offended again despite earlier leniency. Alongside the family’s continuing need for support, the submissions need to address why the offending recurred and the concrete measures actually implemented to prevent it from happening again.
