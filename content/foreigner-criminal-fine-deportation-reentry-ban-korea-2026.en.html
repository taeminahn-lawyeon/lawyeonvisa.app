Criminal Fines, Deportation and Entry-Ban Relief for Foreigners in Korea

Category Criminal · Immigration ReviewReading time approx. 8 min

A criminal fine does not mean that every foreign national will be removed from Korea. However, fine amounts and the number of past penalties can bring a person within the removal criteria, affecting residence and re-entry even where imprisonment is avoided. Removal involves both the criteria applicable to the criminal proceedings and the circumstances supporting continued residence.

For a foreign national facing criminal proceedings, the sentence and the ability to continue living in Korea are connected. An immigration violation review, a departure or deportation order, an entry ban, and re-entry procedures may follow the criminal case. Each procedure has distinct criteria and consequences, so representation considers how the result at one stage affects the next.

Removal criteria for foreign nationals with criminal fines

The Ministry of Justice’s review guidelines for foreign nationals with final criminal fines, as cited in court decisions, establish removal as the default for specified fine amounts or repeated penalties. The principal basic criteria are as follows.

Principal basic criteria concerning fines and repeated penalties
CategoryCriterion for removal
Individual fineA final criminal fine of KRW 3 million or more
Cumulative finesCriminal fines totaling KRW 5 million or more within the preceding five years
Number of penaltiesA fine or a more severe criminal penalty on two or more occasions within the preceding two years, or three or more occasions within the preceding five years

These are alternative criteria; they do not all have to be met. Meeting any one can bring a person within the removal criteria. Even individual fines below KRW 3 million can meet the cumulative-amount or frequency criteria, so the fine in a single case does not fully explain the consequences for residence.

Supplementary criteria also cover three or more outcomes within the preceding five years such as a disposition of no authority to prosecute, suspension of prosecution, dismissal of prosecution, or suspended imposition of sentence, where the repetition indicates a lack of compliance with the law. Repetition in this context does not have the same meaning as the habitual-offending requirement for an aggravated criminal penalty. An outcome that does not result in a final fine can therefore still raise immigration issues under this separate criterion.

Separate criteria may apply to Road Traffic Act violations or sexual violence offences. Both the basic and supplementary criteria need not be met for those separate criteria to apply. Immigration penalty notices also differ from criminal fines in their nature and governing rules; they are not added together under the same fine-amount criterion.

The criminal judgment and the immigration review

These review guidelines are internal standards for the immigration authority’s exercise of discretion, rather than statutory conditions that automatically produce a departure order. This does not mean the authority can disregard them at will. Courts recognize that such guidelines may be respected unless they violate the Constitution or legislation or have lost objective reasonableness.

Where a criterion is met, the nature and seriousness of the offence, the severity and repetition of penalties, the risk of reoffending, and potential harm to public safety and social order are relevant. Family relationships and an established life in Korea, long lawful residence, economic contributions, and humanitarian circumstances may also bear on the need to remain. No single circumstance determines the outcome.

The criminal court determines criminal liability and sentencing. The immigration review takes the criminal judgment and disposition history as its basis for deciding immigration measures. Restitution, settlement, and other sentencing considerations are addressed during criminal defense; once the judgment is final, removal criteria apply to that result. Leniency in the criminal proceedings does not itself produce the same outcome in immigration proceedings or satisfy the requirements for continued residence.

A suspended prison sentence does not guarantee permission to remain. In particular, a prison sentence for a sexual violence offence remains subject to the separate immigration criteria and exemption requirements even where settlement and remorse contributed to suspension of the sentence.

Grounds supporting continued residence and entry-ban exemptions

Even where removal criteria are met, national-interest or humanitarian grounds may support continued residence or an entry-ban exemption. The applicable exception requirements and the need for residence or entry are considered together. The existence of family or a business in Korea does not itself establish an exception.

Family circumstances include relationships with Korean nationals or permanent residents, actual family life, care of minor children, and an established life in Korea. Treatment or family support requires a present need. Past treatment for an industrial injury does not establish that treatment in Korea is still necessary. Where a family member is ill, the seriousness of the condition and the need for care must substantiate why the person’s presence in Korea is needed.

National-interest considerations include investment meeting applicable requirements, domestic business operations, tax payments and economic contributions, and contributions through specialized knowledge or technical skills. The nature of the person’s residence status may also be relevant. Business registration or employment alone does not guarantee permission to remain; actual business activities and the need for residence are assessed against the applicable criteria.

The weight of these circumstances depends on the offence and the disposition history. If a person offends again after receiving permission to remain or making a compliance pledge, family ties and a business base may be insufficient to avoid removal. In a sexual violence case resulting in a prison sentence, long residence, family cohabitation, and grounds for sentencing leniency likewise do not substitute for the exemption requirements.

Representation in the immigration review identifies the criteria applicable to the penalty history and explains how the facts supporting residence relate to an exception. Legal submissions and evidence are prepared accordingly, with responses to requests for additional material during review. The review and approval procedures also depend on whether the result sought is continued residence or an entry-ban exemption.

Different effects of departure orders, residence permission, and entry-ban relief

Where a person subject to deportation intends to leave voluntarily at their own expense, a departure order may be issued instead. Deportation involves compulsory removal by the state, while a departure order requires the person to leave voluntarily by a specified deadline. Although a departure order is a less severe measure, the obligation to leave remains.

Effects of decisions concerning residence, departure, and re-entry
DecisionDirect effect
Permission to remainAllows continued residence in Korea. Lifting an existing entry ban is a separate matter.
Departure order instead of deportationProvides a deadline for voluntary departure. It does not allow continued residence.
Entry-ban exemption or liftingConcerns the restriction on return. It does not itself revoke a departure order or grant a visa or admission.

If a departure order remains in force, the person remains obliged to leave by its deadline even where an entry-ban exemption is granted. Continuing life in Korea then involves departure and subsequent re-entry. Conversely, permission to remain does not itself remove an entry ban that has already been imposed.

An unexpired period of stay also does not extinguish a departure order. Once such an order is issued, its departure deadline applies rather than the expiry date of the previous period of stay.

Revocation, amendment, and challenges after a disposition

Where a departure or deportation order has already been issued, representation addresses the basis of the disposition and the assessment made at that time. Issues can include an incorrect penalty record, application of the wrong criteria, omitted residence or exemption grounds, and whether the discretionary assessment lawfully considered the disadvantages of departure.

A court does not regard compliance with an internal criterion as a complete explanation of the legality of a departure order. It considers public-safety and social-order risks, the need for residence, and the disadvantages of departure when reviewing whether the disposition exceeded or abused the authority’s discretion. However, immigration and residence management involve broad discretion, so economic disadvantage or family ties alone do not establish grounds for revocation.

Revoking or amending an existing removal measure and lifting an entry ban each require the relevant procedure. A request for reconsideration based on factual error or omitted exemption grounds identifies the problem in the original assessment and the reason for changing it. An officer’s statement that the case will be reconsidered, or submission of a request to lift the ban, does not itself change the disposition.

Challenge procedures also depend on the disposition. The Immigration Act objection period for a deportation order is seven days from receipt; the same objection procedure does not apply to departure orders. As a general rule, an action for revocation must be filed within 90 days of learning of the disposition and within one year of the disposition itself. The starting point may differ, including where an administrative appeal has been pursued. Filing an action does not automatically suspend enforcement, so the departure deadline and any application for a stay of execution are addressed together.

Representation concerning a disposition identifies the decision to be revoked or amended, the need for entry-ban relief, available challenge procedures, and the departure deadline. These matters define the work involved in seeking continued residence or pursuing re-entry after departure.

Entry-ban relief and re-entry after departure

For a person who has already left Korea, the remaining entry restriction and the procedures for return become central. Seeking an entry-ban exemption before departure differs from seeking special relief to return during a restriction period after departure. Exemptions are also sometimes called special relief in practice, so the applicable procedure depends on the existing disposition and whether the person has departed, as well as the terminology used.

Expiry of a restriction period or lifting an entry ban does not guarantee admission. Where a visa is required, the requirements for the intended purpose of entry still apply, and immigration inspection considers other grounds for refusing entry. An entry-ban relief decision does not replace a visa or admission decision.

Representation extending to re-entry connects entry-ban relief with subsequent visa and entry procedures for the purpose of the engagement, such as resuming family life, continuing a business, or obtaining treatment. Where representation began with criminal defense, the judgment and the outcomes of the immigration review and removal proceedings inform later work. For a person already abroad, the engagement centers on the remaining restriction and the procedures needed for return.
