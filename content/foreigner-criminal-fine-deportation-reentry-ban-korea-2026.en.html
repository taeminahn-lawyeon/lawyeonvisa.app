Criminal Fines, Deportation and Entry-Ban Relief for Foreigners in Korea

Category Criminal · Immigration ReviewReading time approx. 8 min

A criminal fine or suspended sentence imposed on a foreign national can lead to removal measures and an entry ban. Following a final criminal judgment, the immigration violation review applies immigration criteria to that outcome. Grounds for leniency in criminal proceedings differ from the grounds for permitting continued residence or granting an entry-ban exemption.

Representation spanning criminal defense, immigration review, entry-ban relief, and re-entry involves different objectives at each stage. Criminal proceedings address criminal liability and sentencing. After the judgment becomes final, the work concerns the removal criteria and exceptions applicable to that outcome. Permission to remain in Korea and permission to seek return after departure also have different effects.

Criminal fines, suspended sentences, and removal

A criminal fine does not guarantee continued residence merely because it avoids imprisonment. Removal criteria apply to the offence established by the final judgment, the fine amount, and the cumulative record of punishment. Even a fine below the general threshold can lead to removal where separate criteria apply, including for sexual violence offences.

A suspended prison sentence does not eliminate immigration consequences. In particular, a prison sentence for a sexual violence offence can lead to removal and an entry ban even if its execution is suspended. Leniency based on settlement or remorse in the criminal proceedings does not substitute for the requirements for an immigration exemption.

Requirements for continued residence and entry-ban exemptions

Permission to remain or an entry-ban exemption in a case subject to removal depends on the applicable exception requirements. Sentencing considerations in the criminal proceedings do not carry over as grounds for an exemption under immigration criteria. The issues are whether the person falls within the applicable exemption criteria and whether national-interest or humanitarian grounds justify continued residence or entry.

Humanitarian grounds include certain family relationships with Korean nationals or permanent residents, the circumstances of a person caring for a minor child with an established life in Korea, pregnancy, and necessary medical treatment in Korea. Living with family or having spent a long period in Korea does not establish permission to remain in every case. In particular, for a person sentenced to imprisonment for a sexual violence offence, long residence and family cohabitation alone do not substitute for the exemption requirements.

Where the grounds concern treatment or family support, a present need requires substantiation. Past outpatient treatment for an industrial injury does not establish a current need for treatment in Korea. A medical certificate issued to a family member also raises the question of whether, in light of the condition and treatment period, the person's continued presence in Korea is indispensable to supporting that family member.

National-interest grounds include investment meeting specified requirements, domestic business operations, and the need for a person with specialized knowledge or advanced technical skills to remain in Korea. Employment or business registration alone does not establish those grounds. The relevant requirements and the need for residence require factual support.

Representation in an immigration review identifies the criteria and exception requirements applicable to the final judgment and establishes the facts supporting those requirements. Legal submissions and evidence are prepared against those requirements, and the respective review and approval procedures for continued residence and entry-ban exemption are pursued.

A departure order still requires departure

Where a person subject to deportation intends to leave voluntarily, a departure order may be issued instead of a deportation order. Deportation involves compulsory removal by the state; a departure order imposes an obligation to leave voluntarily by a specified deadline. Changing the measure to a departure order does not permit continued residence.

An entry-ban exemption also differs from revocation of a departure order. If the departure order remains in force, the obligation to leave remains even when the entry ban is exempted. Return to Korea then becomes an issue after departure. Conversely, permission to remain does not itself remove an entry ban that has already been imposed.

An unexpired period of stay does not eliminate grounds for removal. Where a departure order has been issued, its departure deadline applies, rather than the expiry date of the previous period of stay. Assuming that departure can be addressed when the next extension is sought overlooks the existing obligation to leave.

Changing an existing disposition and lifting an entry ban

Once a departure order and entry ban have been imposed, the procedure differs from requesting leniency in an initial review. Where exemption grounds existed but the facts were misunderstood or omitted, the relevant factual error and its connection to an exemption requirement form the basis for seeking revocation or amendment of the disposition and lifting the ban.

A decision changing an existing removal measure and a decision lifting an entry ban each require the relevant authority and review and approval procedures. An officer's statement that the case will be reconsidered, or the submission of a request for relief, does not itself extinguish the existing disposition. The obligation to depart and the restriction on return depend on which decisions have actually been revoked or amended.

Treating an existing disposition as a mere data-entry error also does not substitute for a lawful relief procedure. A request to revoke or amend a disposition presents the original decision and the actual grounds for changing it, while pursuing continued residence and entry-ban relief through the respective procedures.

A challenge to the legality of a disposition also differs from entry-ban relief. The Immigration Act objection period for a deportation order is seven days from receipt; the same objection procedure does not apply to departure orders. As a general rule, the filing periods for an action for revocation are 90 days from learning of the disposition and one year from the disposition itself. The starting point may differ, including where an administrative appeal has been pursued. Filing an action does not automatically suspend enforcement.

Entry-ban relief and re-entry after departure

After a person subject to an entry ban has left Korea, relief concerns the ability to return. Before departure, the review concerns the entry-ban exemption requirements; where return is sought during a restriction period after departure, special relief is considered. Entry-ban exemptions are also sometimes called special relief in practice, so terminology alone does not identify the applicable procedure.

Lifting an entry ban and securing actual admission are separate stages. Even after relief, the person remains subject to any applicable visa requirements for the intended purpose of entry and to immigration inspection. An entry-ban relief decision does not itself grant a visa or admission.

Representation extending to re-entry continues beyond lifting the entry ban. Subsequent visa and entry procedures are connected to the purpose of the engagement, such as resuming family life, continuing a business, or obtaining treatment. Where representation began with criminal defense, the final judgment and the outcomes of the immigration review and removal proceedings inform the later stages. For a person already abroad, the work centers on the remaining restriction and the procedures needed for return.
