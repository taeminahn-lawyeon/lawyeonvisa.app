Criminal Fines, Deportation and Entry-Ban Relief for Foreigners in Korea

Category Criminal · Immigration ReviewReading time approx. 8 min

A foreign national's criminal case can continue to affect residence in Korea and future entry after a fine has been paid or a suspended sentence imposed. The facts established in the criminal proceedings and the penalty imposed inform the immigration violation review. Removal and entry restrictions can then determine whether the person can continue living with family or operating a business in Korea.

A reduced criminal penalty and permission to remain in Korea are separate outcomes. The immigration review considers the grounds for continued residence; after a disposition, the obligation to depart and restrictions on future entry remain distinct issues. This relationship informs representation that connects criminal defense during the investigation and trial with continued residence and re-entry.

Criminal fines and the criteria for removal

The following amounts and frequencies have been applied as basic removal criteria in criminal-fine cases.

Basic removal criteria in criminal-fine cases
CategoryBasic removal criterion
Individual criminal fineKRW 3 million or more
Cumulative finesA total of KRW 5 million or more within the preceding five years
Repeated punishmentTwo or more final criminal-fine convictions within the preceding two years, or three or more within the preceding five years

Meeting a criterion brings the person within the scope of removal, but the amount and frequency alone do not determine the disposition. Offence-specific rules and grounds for allowing continued residence also apply. General fine thresholds do not determine the immigration outcome of sexual or drug offences, and Road Traffic Act offences are assessed under separate criteria. A fine below the general threshold can still have immigration consequences because of the offence or the person's record.

A criminal fine also differs from an immigration penalty payment under the notification procedure for immigration-law violations. A criminal fine is a criminal punishment, and different monetary and cumulative thresholds apply to these two measures. A criminal fine falling below an immigration-penalty threshold therefore does not establish permission to remain.

A suspended sentence can still leave grounds for deportation. Even a reduced fine may leave removal risks arising from the offence or the person's record.

Restitution, settlement with a victim, and measures to prevent reoffending are relevant both to sentencing and to the immigration review. Leniency in the criminal proceedings does not itself establish permission to remain in Korea. The immigration review assesses not only the severity of the criminal penalty but also the grounds for continued residence under separate standards.

Grounds for continued residence and entry-ban exemptions

Family relationships, an established life in Korea, medical needs, and national-interest grounds can affect the disposition even where a person meets removal criteria. The issue extends beyond having family or employment in Korea. The nature and severity of the consequences of departure, and the reasons why residence in Korea is necessary, are assessed together with the seriousness of the offence and the risk of reoffending.

In cases concerning the care of a minor child, the actual caregiving arrangements, the availability of another caregiver, and the effects of departure on the child form part of the assessment. Medical treatment or family care raises questions about the treatment currently required, its duration, the need for treatment in Korea, and the need for the person to provide care directly. A past industrial injury or a medical certificate issued to a family member does not, by itself, establish a current need for residence.

National-interest grounds can involve the substance of an investment or business, employment of Korean nationals, specialist skills, and the need for that person to remain in Korea. Business registration or employment records establish that a business or employment relationship exists. Disruption to domestic operations and employment caused by departure can support the reasons why residence is necessary to sustain that relationship.

These circumstances do not produce the same exception for every offence. In a serious case, or where a person has offended again after receiving an immigration warning, existing family and employment circumstances alone may not overcome an adverse assessment of the seriousness and repetition of the conduct. Representation in the immigration review involves identifying applicable exceptions and their limits, then preparing legal submissions and evidence connecting those grounds to the need for continued residence.

Permission to remain, a departure order in place of deportation, and exemption from an entry ban are different outcomes. A departure order still requires departure. An entry-ban exemption does not itself cancel that order. A less restrictive method of departure or an exemption from re-entry restrictions therefore does not itself establish permission to remain in Korea.

Departure orders, deportation and entry-ban periods

A departure order requires the person to leave by a specified deadline. A deportation order provides for compulsory removal from Korea. An entry restriction governs subsequent entry. The restriction depends on the form of removal, the offence and sentence, and any separately applicable grounds for denying entry.

A person who departed under a deportation order less than five years earlier falls within a statutory ground for an entry ban. This does not mean that every restriction arising from a criminal case ends after five years. Separate grounds, including a risk to public safety or social order, may remain relevant. The label 'departure order' likewise does not establish a particular ban period or the absence of a ban.

Even where a departure order is issued instead of deportation, the method of departure and restrictions on future entry are assessed separately. A person needing uninterrupted residence in Korea seeks a different outcome from someone intending to leave and return by a particular time. The former case centers on relief from the removal measure itself; the latter also involves the entry restriction and the requirements for return.

Challenges to a disposition and entry-ban relief

A departure or deportation order can be challenged on grounds such as a material factual error, an error in applying the law, or an excessive exercise of discretion. The Immigration Act objection period for a deportation order is seven days from receipt of the order. This procedure concerns deportation orders; the same objection procedure does not apply to departure orders. As a general rule, the filing periods for an action for revocation are 90 days from learning of the disposition and one year from the disposition itself. The starting point may differ, including where an administrative appeal has been pursued. Filing an action does not automatically suspend enforcement.

Entry-ban relief addresses the restriction preventing return to Korea. Ordinary lifting follows expiry of the restriction period or other cessation of its grounds. Special relief seeks an exception on national-interest or humanitarian grounds while the restriction remains in force. Ordinary lifting concerns the end of the restriction; special relief involves an assessment of the need to permit entry during that period.

Grounds for special relief can concern family reunification and the care of minor children in Korea, necessary medical treatment or family care, an established life in Korea, investment and business activities, or the need for specialist personnel. The assessment considers both the relationship and the concrete need to permit entry during the restriction period. The seriousness of the offence, risk of reoffending, subsequent circumstances, and compliance with the law are also relevant.

Where circumstances existing at the time of the disposition were omitted or incorrectly assessed, the challenge addresses the original error and its effect. Changes in medical or caregiving needs after departure can support a current need for entry. Grounds for special relief do not necessarily have to arise for the first time after departure. When previously assessed circumstances are presented again, the issue is why entry is now justified despite the earlier assessment.

Even if an entry ban is exempted or lifted before departure, the obligation to depart remains if the departure order is maintained. For a person seeking continued residence, the procedures pursued depend on the respective effects of revoking or changing the existing removal measure and lifting the entry ban.

Re-entry after an entry ban is lifted

Lifting an entry ban does not erase the criminal conviction or removal history. The requirements for the visa sought, including separate restrictions based on criminal history, can still apply, and entry requirements are assessed at the point of arrival. Expiry of a ban or approval of special relief therefore does not guarantee admission.

Resuming family life, continuing a business, and entering temporarily for treatment involve different purposes and periods of residence. Representation extending to re-entry connects the proposed activities presented in the entry-ban relief application with the subsequent visa application, while addressing the effects of the criminal conviction and removal history in each procedure.
