Criminal Fines, Deportation and Entry-Ban Relief for Foreigners in Korea

Category Criminal · Immigration ReviewReading time approx. 8 min

A foreign national's criminal case can continue to affect residence in Korea and future entry after a fine has been paid or a suspended sentence imposed. The facts established in the criminal proceedings and the penalty imposed inform the immigration violation review. Removal and entry restrictions can then determine whether the person can continue living with family or operating a business in Korea.

Criminal defense for a foreign national who needs to remain in Korea must therefore account for immigration consequences from the investigation and trial stages. During the immigration review, grounds for allowing continued residence and grounds for exempting the person from an entry ban require distinct submissions. Once a disposition has been issued, challenges to that disposition and applications for entry-ban relief serve different purposes.

Criminal fines and the criteria for removal

The following amounts and frequencies have been applied as basic removal criteria in criminal-fine cases.

Basic removal criteria in criminal-fine cases
CategoryBasic removal criterion
Individual criminal fineKRW 3 million or more
Cumulative finesA total of KRW 5 million or more within the preceding five years
Repeated punishmentTwo or more final criminal-fine convictions within the preceding two years, or three or more within the preceding five years

Meeting a criterion brings the person within the scope of removal, but the amount and frequency alone do not determine the disposition. Offence-specific rules and grounds for allowing continued residence also apply. General fine thresholds do not determine the immigration outcome of sexual or drug offences, and Road Traffic Act offences are assessed under separate criteria. A fine below the general threshold does not eliminate every possible immigration consequence.

A criminal fine also differs from an immigration penalty notice. A criminal fine is a criminal punishment; an immigration penalty notice is issued under the notification procedure for immigration-law violations. Different monetary and cumulative thresholds apply. The amount of a criminal fine cannot be assessed against the thresholds for an immigration penalty notice.

At the criminal defense stage, the likely sentence must be considered alongside cumulative prior penalties, the facts that may be established, and the immigration criteria applicable to the offence. Avoiding immediate imprisonment through a suspended sentence can still leave grounds for deportation. Focusing solely on obtaining a fine below a particular amount can overlook removal risks arising from the offence or the person's record.

Restitution, settlement with a victim, and measures to prevent reoffending can be relevant both to sentencing and to the immigration review. Leniency in the criminal proceedings does not itself establish permission to remain in Korea. Once the criminal outcome is determined, the reasons for allowing continued residence must be presented under the standards governing the immigration review.

Grounds for continued residence and entry-ban exemptions

Family relationships, an established life in Korea, medical needs, and national-interest grounds can affect the disposition even where a person meets removal criteria. The issue extends beyond having family or employment in Korea. The nature and severity of the consequences of departure, and the reasons why residence in Korea is necessary, are assessed together with the seriousness of the offence and the risk of reoffending.

Where the grounds concern the care of a minor child, the actual caregiving arrangements, the availability of another caregiver, and the consequences for the child require explanation. Medical treatment or family care raises questions about the treatment currently required, its duration, why it must continue in Korea, and why the person's own presence as a caregiver is necessary. A past industrial injury or a medical certificate issued to a family member does not, by itself, establish those needs.

National-interest grounds can involve the substance of an investment or business, employment of Korean nationals, specialist skills, and the need for that person to remain in Korea. Evidence of business registration or employment has a different significance from an explanation of how departure would disrupt domestic operations and employment.

These circumstances do not produce the same exception for every offence. In a serious case, or where a person has offended again after receiving an immigration warning, repeating existing family and employment circumstances has limits if the seriousness and recurrence of the conduct remain unaddressed.

Permission to remain, a departure order in place of deportation, and exemption from an entry ban are different outcomes. A departure order still requires departure. An entry-ban exemption does not itself cancel that order. Where continued residence in Korea is the objective, the disposition sought must address residence itself, beyond securing a less restrictive method of departure.

Departure orders, deportation and entry-ban periods

A departure order requires the person to leave by a specified deadline. A deportation order provides for compulsory removal from Korea. An entry restriction governs subsequent entry. The restriction depends on the form of removal, the offence and sentence, and any separately applicable grounds for denying entry.

A person who departed under a deportation order less than five years earlier falls within a statutory ground for an entry ban. This does not mean that every restriction arising from a criminal case ends after five years. Separate grounds, including a risk to public safety or social order, may remain relevant. The label 'departure order' likewise does not establish a particular ban period or the absence of a ban.

This distinction matters when accepting a departure order as a less severe disposition. The method of departure and restrictions on future entry remain separate decisions. The grounds advanced and the outcome sought depend on whether the person needs uninterrupted residence in Korea or can leave but must return by a particular time.

Challenges to a disposition and entry-ban relief

A departure or deportation order can be challenged on grounds such as a material factual error, an error in applying the law, or an excessive exercise of discretion. The Immigration Act objection against a deportation order must be filed within seven days of receipt of the order. That deadline does not apply in the same way to a departure order. As a general rule, an action for revocation must comply with the filing periods of 90 days from learning of the disposition and one year from the disposition itself. Filing an action does not automatically suspend enforcement.

Entry-ban relief addresses the restriction preventing return to Korea. Ordinary lifting concerns the expiry of the restriction period or other cessation of its grounds. Special relief seeks an exception on national-interest or humanitarian grounds while the restriction remains in force. These are not two interchangeable applications for shortening a ban.

Grounds for special relief can concern family reunification and the care of minor children in Korea, necessary medical treatment or family care, an established life in Korea, investment and business activities, or the need for specialist personnel. Beyond the existence of a family or business relationship, there must be a concrete reason to permit entry during the restriction period. The seriousness of the offence, risk of reoffending, subsequent circumstances, and compliance with the law are also relevant.

A case in which existing circumstances were omitted or incorrectly assessed calls for a different submission from one involving medical or caregiving needs arising after departure. The former addresses the original error and its effect; the latter addresses the present need for entry. Grounds for special relief do not necessarily have to arise for the first time after departure.

If an entry ban is exempted or lifted before departure, that outcome must still be distinguished from permission to remain in Korea. Where an obligation to depart remains, relief from the entry ban alone does not resolve continued residence.

Re-entry after an entry ban is lifted

Lifting an entry ban does not erase the criminal conviction or removal history. The requirements for the visa sought, including separate restrictions based on criminal history, can still apply. The person must also meet the requirements at the point of entry. Expiry of a ban or approval of special relief does not guarantee admission.

Where the engagement aims to secure re-entry, its scope should address visa issuance and admission requirements after entry-ban relief. The purpose of the proposed activities in Korea presented in the relief application must be consistent with the subsequent visa application. Resuming family life, continuing a business, and entering temporarily for treatment involve different residence outcomes.
