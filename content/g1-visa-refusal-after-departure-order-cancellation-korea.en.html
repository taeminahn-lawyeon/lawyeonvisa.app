G-1 Visa Refusal After a Departure Order Was Set Aside: Why the Court Cancelled the Refusal

Law Firm Lawyeon2026.09.18

Contents

1. Key Issues2. Implications

Seoul Administrative Court, November 2, 2022, Case No. 2022Gudan53597

An asylum seeker whose departure order had been set aside applied to change to G-1-5 status, but the immigration authority refused the application because of the applicant’s history of unauthorized employment. The court rejected the argument that the refusal was null and void because it violated the binding effect of the earlier judgment. However, after examining the circumstances of the unauthorized employment and delayed asylum application, the public benefit of withholding immigration status, and the alternative of scrutinizing an application for permission to work, the court set aside the refusal as an unlawful exercise of discretion.

1. Key Issues

(a) Whether the judgment setting aside the departure order bound the authority when deciding the subsequent application to change status

The applicant entered Korea under B-1 visa-exempt status, worked at a massage establishment, and filed a first asylum application after overstaying by approximately one month and 16 days. The immigration authority issued a departure order because of the overstay, but that order was set aside in separate proceedings, and the judgment became final. The applicant then applied to change to G-1-5 status. The authority refused on the ground that the requirements were not met. Information disclosure requests revealed the specific reason: the authority considered the applicant’s conduct during their stay unsatisfactory because they had entered under a status that did not permit employment and then worked unlawfully at a massage establishment.

The applicant argued that withholding immigration status despite the earlier judgment violated that judgment’s binding effect. The court held that the departure order and the refusal to change status were separate administrative decisions with different requirements, purposes, and effects. The subsequent refusal therefore did not violate the binding effect of the earlier judgment. The court dismissed the claim for a declaration of nullity and examined, under the alternative claim for cancellation, whether the refusal exceeded or abused the authority’s discretion.

(b) Whether unauthorized employment and an overstay sufficiently justified refusing G-1 status

The court held that applying for asylum does not automatically confer G-1 status. The authority retains discretion to assess the applicant’s eligibility, purpose of stay, and impact on the public interest. In this case, however, the court did not consider the existence of unauthorized employment and an overstay sufficient to justify withholding status.

This was the applicant’s first asylum application. The applicant had neither applied shortly before the expiry of an authorized stay after an extended period in Korea nor applied to delay the enforcement of a deportation order. The claim that family members had assaulted and confined the applicant after they resisted a forced marriage was not, on its face, outside the grounds that could support refugee recognition. In these circumstances, the court found that filing after an overstay did not, by itself, establish abuse of the asylum system.

The court also examined the circumstances of the employment. It accepted that the applicant had worked at the establishment and considered that prostitution appeared to have occurred. The applicant stated, however, that they had not known the establishment provided sexual services when they took the job, that their passport had been confiscated, and that the owner’s coercion and confinement had forced them into unwanted prostitution before they escaped. In light of those statements and the circumstances of the applicant’s employment and departure from the establishment, the court found mitigating circumstances for the delayed asylum application. It also found insufficient grounds to conclude that G-1 status should be withheld because the applicant posed a risk to public order or public morals. Those circumstances had already been examined in the earlier departure-order proceedings, and no new evidence or other basis for a different assessment emerged in the present case.

(c) Whether withholding immigration status was justified when the concern could be addressed through scrutiny of permission to work

The applicant’s challenge to the denial of refugee status was pending on appeal. The court considered that, even if G-1 status was refused, the departure deadline would continue to be deferred. The refusal would therefore be unlikely to achieve its purpose until the asylum proceedings were finally resolved. In the meantime, the applicant would remain in a precarious position without immigration status.

The court held that, if it was necessary to prevent use of the asylum system to extend a stay for employment, strict scrutiny of an application for permission to work could address that concern. Refusing a change of status imposed a greater disadvantage and was a disproportionate response. The court set aside the refusal because the public benefit it sought to achieve did not outweigh the harm to the applicant.

2. Implications

Reliance on the binding effect of the earlier departure-order judgment did not establish that the subsequent refusal was unlawful. Nevertheless, the circumstances of the employment and the statements concerning coercion and confinement considered in that judgment remained relevant to whether the applicant posed a risk to immigration control. When an earlier successful judgment is relied on in subsequent proceedings, the issues may therefore include both the decision that the judgment set aside and how the facts it established or assessed undermine the grounds for the new decision.

The applicant also succeeded in challenging the refusal despite the court’s acceptance that unauthorized employment had occurred. The circumstances leading to that employment and the statement about passport confiscation affected the assessment of both the employment history and the delayed asylum application. The fact that this was a first application, together with the substance of the alleged persecution, provided grounds to contest the characterization of the application as an abuse of the system. Identifying the specific reasons behind the notice’s general statement that the requirements were not met allowed the applicant to challenge both the factual basis and the assessment underlying the authority’s adverse view of their conduct during their stay.

The availability of separate scrutiny of permission to work also contributed to the decision to set aside the refusal. In particular, the pending asylum proceedings meant that deferrals of the departure deadline were expected to continue even if immigration status was withheld. The refusal was unlikely to secure departure, while the employment activities of concern could be controlled through a separate permission process. Together, those circumstances undermined the necessity of refusing immigration status altogether. When relying on this judgment in comparable cases, the public benefit the refusal can achieve in practice, and the availability of other means of securing that benefit, are central to an argument that the refusal is disproportionate.
