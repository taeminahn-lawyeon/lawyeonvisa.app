G-1 visa refusal after cancellation of a departure order

Law Firm Lawyeon2026.09.18

Contents

1. Key issues2. Implications

Seoul Administrative Court, November 2, 2022, Case No. 2022Gudan53597

An asylum seeker whose departure order had been set aside applied to change to G-1-5 status, but the immigration authority refused the application because of the applicant’s history of unauthorized employment. The court rejected the claim that the new refusal was void because it violated the earlier judgment’s binding effect. It nevertheless found an abuse of discretion, considering the circumstances of the employment and delayed asylum application, the public benefit of refusing status, and the availability of work-permit scrutiny to prevent misuse of the asylum system.

1. Key issues

(a) Binding effect of the earlier judgment

The applicant entered Korea under B-1 visa-exempt status, worked at a massage establishment, and filed a first asylum application after overstaying by approximately one month and 16 days. The immigration authority issued a departure order because of the overstay, but that order was set aside in separate proceedings, and the judgment became final. The applicant then applied to change to G-1-5 status. The authority refused on the ground that the requirements were not met. Information disclosure requests revealed the specific reason: the authority considered the applicant’s conduct during their stay unsatisfactory because they had entered under a status that did not permit employment and then worked unlawfully at a massage establishment.

The applicant argued that withholding immigration status despite the earlier judgment violated that judgment’s binding effect. The court held that the departure order and the refusal to change status were separate administrative decisions with different requirements, purposes, and effects. The subsequent refusal therefore did not violate the binding effect of the earlier judgment. The court dismissed the claim for a declaration of nullity and examined, under the alternative claim for cancellation, whether the refusal exceeded or abused the authority’s discretion.

(b) Circumstances of unauthorized employment and overstay

The court held that applying for asylum does not automatically confer G-1 status. The authority retains discretion to assess the applicant’s eligibility, purpose of stay, and impact on the public interest. In this case, however, the unauthorized employment and overstay were insufficient grounds to refuse G-1 status.

This was the applicant’s first asylum application. The applicant had neither applied shortly before the expiry of an authorized stay after an extended period in Korea nor applied to delay the enforcement of a deportation order. The applicant’s account of assault and confinement by family members after resisting a forced marriage could potentially support refugee recognition. In these circumstances, the court found that filing after an overstay did not, by itself, establish abuse of the asylum system.

The court also examined the circumstances of the employment. It accepted that the applicant had worked at the establishment and considered that prostitution appeared to have occurred. The applicant stated, however, that they had not known the establishment provided sexual services when they took the job, that their passport had been confiscated, and that the owner’s coercion and confinement had forced them into unwanted prostitution before they escaped. In light of those statements and the circumstances of the applicant’s employment and departure from the establishment, the court found mitigating circumstances for the delayed asylum application. It also found insufficient grounds to conclude that G-1 status should be withheld because the applicant posed a risk to public order or public morals. Those circumstances had already been examined in the earlier departure-order proceedings, and no new evidence or other basis for a different assessment emerged in the present case.

(c) Proportionality of the status refusal

The applicant’s challenge to the denial of refugee status was pending on appeal. The court expected the departure deadline to be deferred until the asylum proceedings concluded, even if G-1 status was refused. Refusing status was therefore unlikely to secure the applicant’s departure. In the meantime, the applicant would remain in a precarious position without immigration status.

The court held that, if it was necessary to prevent use of the asylum system to extend a stay for employment, strict scrutiny of an application for permission to work could address that concern. Refusing a change of status imposed a greater disadvantage and was a disproportionate response. The court set aside the refusal because the public benefit it sought to achieve did not outweigh the harm to the applicant.

2. Implications

Where an applicant wins a challenge to a departure order but is subsequently refused immigration status because of the same violations, the earlier court’s assessment of the circumstances and risks can support an abuse-of-discretion challenge to the new refusal. If the court considered circumstances favorable to the applicant, such as coercion in obtaining or continuing employment, but the authority disregarded them and relied solely on the employment record, the explanations and evidence accepted in the earlier proceedings can rebut that assessment. Any subsequent violation or new evidence would also need to be addressed. A challenge to the later refusal can therefore identify the facts the authority omitted or misjudged, rather than rely solely on the existence of the earlier judgment.

For an applicant refused G-1 status during asylum litigation, the disadvantages of remaining without status despite a deferred departure deadline can also support a claim for cancellation. Where further deferrals are expected until the asylum proceedings conclude and there are grounds against treating the application as abusive, the applicant can question what public benefit withholding status would actually achieve. If the authority’s concern is misuse of the asylum system for employment, the availability of work-permit scrutiny can support the argument that refusing immigration status is disproportionate. This argument depends on the circumstances of the asylum application, the prospect of continued departure-deadline deferrals, and whether work-permit scrutiny offers an effective alternative in that case.
