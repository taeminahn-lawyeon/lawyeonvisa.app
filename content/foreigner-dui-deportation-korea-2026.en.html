Foreigner DUI Fines in Korea: Deportation Criteria and the Immigration Review

Category Immigration · CriminalReading time approx. 7 min

A foreign national’s DUI case can create residence issues in addition to criminal punishment and driving-licence measures. Even a case that ends with a fine may lead to a removal issue in the immigration review, affecting family life or business in Korea. This is why subsequent immigration proceedings matter from the criminal-defense stage.

The DUI fine is a criminal outcome that connects to criteria applied in a later immigration review. Its amount does not, however, automatically determine whether a departure order or deportation follows. The applicable offence and punishment criteria, the need to remain and the effect of the actual decision are separate matters.

DUI penalties and the issues in the criminal case

Issues in a DUI case include whether driving occurred, blood alcohol concentration, the testing process, any accident and prior punishment. The statutory ranges are a starting point; the actual penalty depends on the offence charged and the facts.

Blood alcohol concentrationStatutory penalty for ordinary DUI
0.03% to below 0.08%Up to one year of imprisonment or a fine of up to KRW 5 million
0.08% to below 0.2%One to two years of imprisonment or a fine of KRW 5–10 million
0.2% or aboveTwo to five years of imprisonment or a fine of KRW 10–20 million

The table concerns ordinary DUI involving motor vehicles and other covered vehicles, excluding personal mobility devices. Separate provisions may apply to repeat offences, refusal of or interference with alcohol testing, and accidents causing injury or death. Blood alcohol concentration alone cannot determine the sentence or immigration outcome.

How a criminal fine connects to removal criteria

The criteria for foreign nationals with final criminal fines cited in judgments include a fine of at least KRW 3 million, cumulative fines of at least KRW 5 million within five years, or a fine or more severe punishment at least twice within two years or three times within five years. Meeting one criterion may place a person within a category for which removal is the general principle.

This is not a table assigning a departure order to KRW 3 million and deportation to KRW 5 million. The criteria guide the exercise of administrative discretion. Meeting a criterion and the lawfulness of the actual decision are distinct questions.

Repeated punishments may meet a criterion even if individual fines are small. Conversely, a first DUI does not remove the immigration issue created by a fine reaching the relevant amount. Whether separate offence-specific criteria apply also matters.

These are court-imposed criminal fines. Immigration penalties and administrative fines cannot be treated as the same criminal punishment when counting amounts and occurrences.

Criminal sentencing and the immigration assessment

Restitution, settlement and remorse may be addressed in sentencing submissions. The resulting criminal punishment forms the basis for the immigration assessment, but factors favorable at sentencing are not identical to grounds for permission to remain or exemption from an entry ban.

An immigration review concerns the applicable removal criteria and exceptions, the nature and repetition of the offence, risks to public safety and social order, and the need to remain in Korea. A fine or suspended sentence in the criminal case does not itself establish permission to continue residing here.

Integrated representation therefore involves more than sending the same letter of remorse to several authorities. Criminal proceedings address the allegation and punishment; immigration proceedings address the criteria applicable to that outcome, residence needs and the propriety of the decision.

Family life, business and the need to remain

Family relationships, care of minor children, an established life in Korea, treatment needs and business operations can explain the harm caused by departure and the need for residence. Living with family or having operated a business for a long time does not itself guarantee permission to remain.

DUI cases raise road-safety and reoffending concerns. A further offence after earlier leniency or a pledge to comply with the law can change the assessment even where family and economic circumstances remain.

Responding to removal and an entry ban

After a removal decision, the grounds for cancellation or amendment and the appropriate challenge are examined. The Immigration Control Act objection against a deportation order is filed within seven days of receipt; the same procedure does not apply to a departure order. Revocation proceedings and suspension of enforcement each have their own requirements.

A lawsuit does not itself suspend the obligation to leave or enforcement. If a departure order is issued, its departure deadline matters even if time remains on the previous permission to stay.

An exemption from or lifting of an entry ban does not remove the obligation to leave while a departure order remains. After departure, re-entry work connects the remaining restriction and grounds for relief with visa and admission requirements.
