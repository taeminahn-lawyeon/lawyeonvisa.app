F-6 visa extension for marital breakdown before divorce

Seoul Administrative Court, August 13, 2026, Case No. 2025Gudan53041

The immigration authority refused an F-6 extension for a foreign national who had lived apart from his Korean spouse for approximately thirteen years, citing a lack of a genuine marital relationship. The court agreed that normal married life had ended. It nevertheless set aside the refusal because the Korean spouse bore primary responsibility for the breakdown and a final divorce was not a requirement for F-6-3 status.

1. Key issues

(a) Timing of the F-6-1 eligibility assessment

The applicant relied on principles governing the validity of a marriage, arguing that the marriage had been genuine when it began. The court held that the legality of an extension refusal must be assessed as of the date of that decision. The family-law inquiry into the parties’ intention at the time of marriage did not determine their eligibility for an immigration extension years later.

During their lengthy separation, the couple had not established or maintained shared marital assets and had had little contact. The Korean spouse had withdrawn her sponsorship, had not renewed it, and stated that she did not intend to resume living together and wanted a divorce. The court viewed the applicant’s payments to his spouse as child support, rather than wider financial support for the spouse. Taken together, these facts established that normal married life had ended and that the applicant did not meet the F-6-1 requirements applicable to the spouse of a Korean national.

(b) Primary responsibility for the breakdown

F-6-3 covers a person who, while residing in Korea as the spouse of a Korean national, becomes unable to maintain a normal marital relationship for reasons not attributable to that person. The court held that the foreign spouse did not have to be entirely free from fault. The question was whether the Korean spouse bore primary responsibility for the breakdown. Requiring complete absence of fault could deter a foreign spouse from exercising the right to divorce for fear of losing residence status and could enable the Korean spouse to exploit that vulnerability.

The Korean spouse had unilaterally moved with the child to her parents’ home. The couple’s home and the applicant’s work were in Seoul. Relocating would have involved a substantial change, including living with the spouse’s parents, yet there was insufficient evidence that the parties had meaningfully discussed that change. The applicant continued to propose living together in Seoul. His spouse, however, insisted that she would not return unless he secured an apartment, without regard to the couple’s financial circumstances. The court did not accept that the move had been agreed or that the separation resulted from the applicant breaking a promise to follow her.

The applicant had continued sending child support throughout the separation. The Korean spouse, by contrast, opposed his continued residence in Korea even though the child wished to maintain contact with his father. In light of these circumstances, the court found that the authority’s evidence was insufficient to establish its ground for refusal: that primary responsibility for the marital breakdown did not lie with the Korean spouse.

(c) F-6-3 eligibility before divorce

The immigration authority argued that the status-of-stay manual described marital breakdown by reference to death, disappearance and divorce, and that the applicant could not qualify for F-6-3 because he had not legally divorced. The Enforcement Decree of the Immigration Act, however, refers to an inability to maintain a normal marital relationship and does not require a completed divorce.

The court did not interpret the word “divorce” in the manual’s heading as narrowing the Decree’s scope. The manual imposed no such restriction. Its supporting-document list included a divorce complaint as well as a divorce judgment, allowing marital breakdown to be assessed while divorce proceedings were still pending. Making eligibility depend on a final divorce would also conflict with the purpose of allowing continued residence for a foreign spouse whose marriage had broken down primarily because of the Korean spouse’s conduct.

2. Implications

A foreign spouse staying in Korea under F-6 status whose authorized stay expires during divorce proceedings can request an extension under F-6-3 on the basis that the Korean spouse bears primary responsibility for the marital breakdown. Under this court’s interpretation, the absence of a divorce judgment does not exclude the applicant from assessment under that category. Eligibility to remain on the basis of marital breakdown can therefore be examined before the divorce becomes final. Where married life has already ended, the continued legal existence of the marriage may be insufficient to support an F-6-1 extension. The applicant can instead seek an F-6-3 assessment by presenting the decisions that led to separation, the reasons attempts to live together failed, and the support provided after separation as evidence of responsibility for the breakdown.

In proceedings challenging an extension refusal, the applicant can ask the administrative court to determine responsibility for the marital breakdown without waiting for a finding of fault in the divorce case. The applicant can contest a conclusion that the Korean spouse’s opposition to continued residence, or some fault on the applicant’s part, defeats F-6-3 eligibility. For example, where responsibility is attributed to the foreign spouse for refusing to live together, whether the proposed housing arrangements were feasible given the couple’s finances and employment may change that assessment. By challenging the facts and evaluations underlying the refusal and establishing that the Korean spouse bears primary responsibility, the applicant can seek cancellation on the ground that the authority wrongly found the F-6-3 requirements unmet.
