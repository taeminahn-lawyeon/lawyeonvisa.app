F-6 Visa Extension Before Divorce: Residence Based on Marital Breakdown

Law Firm Lawyeon2026.09.18

Contents

Seoul Administrative Court, August 13, 2026, Case No. 2025Gudan53041

The immigration authority refused an F-6 extension for a foreign national who had lived apart from his Korean spouse for approximately thirteen years, citing a lack of a genuine marital relationship. The court agreed that normal married life had ended. It nevertheless set aside the refusal because the Korean spouse bore primary responsibility for the breakdown and a final divorce was not a requirement for F-6-3 status.

1. Key Issues

(a) Whether a genuine intention to marry at the outset established eligibility for an F-6-1 extension

The applicant relied on principles governing the validity of a marriage, arguing that the marriage had been genuine when it began. The court held that the legality of an extension refusal must be assessed as of the date of that decision. The family-law inquiry into the parties’ intention at the time of marriage did not determine their eligibility for an immigration extension years later.

During their lengthy separation, the couple had not established or maintained shared marital assets and had had little contact. The Korean spouse had withdrawn her sponsorship, had not renewed it, and stated that she did not intend to resume living together and wanted a divorce. The court acknowledged the applicant’s payments to his spouse but did not consider them to exceed the level of child support. Taken together, these facts established that normal married life had ended and that the applicant did not meet the F-6-1 requirements applicable to the spouse of a Korean national.

(b) Who bore primary responsibility for the marital breakdown

F-6-3 covers a person who, while residing in Korea as the spouse of a Korean national, becomes unable to maintain a normal marital relationship for reasons not attributable to that person. The court held that the foreign spouse did not have to be entirely free from fault. The question was whether the Korean spouse bore primary responsibility for the breakdown. Requiring complete absence of fault could deter a foreign spouse from exercising the right to divorce for fear of losing residence status and could enable the Korean spouse to exploit that vulnerability.

The Korean spouse had unilaterally moved with the child to her parents’ home. The couple’s home and the applicant’s work were in Seoul. Relocating would have involved a substantial change, including living with the spouse’s parents, yet there was insufficient evidence that the parties had meaningfully discussed that change. The applicant continued to propose living together in Seoul. His spouse, however, insisted that she would not return unless he secured an apartment, without regard to the couple’s financial circumstances. The court did not accept that the move had been agreed or that the separation resulted from the applicant breaking a promise to follow her.

The applicant had continued sending child support throughout the separation. The Korean spouse, by contrast, opposed his continued residence in Korea even though the child wished to maintain contact with his father. In light of these circumstances, the court found that the authority’s evidence was insufficient to establish its ground for refusal: that primary responsibility for the marital breakdown did not lie with the Korean spouse.

(c) Whether a final divorce was required for F-6-3 eligibility

The immigration authority argued that the status-of-stay manual described marital breakdown by reference to death, disappearance and divorce, and that the applicant could not qualify for F-6-3 because he had not legally divorced. The Enforcement Decree of the Immigration Act, however, refers to an inability to maintain a normal marital relationship and does not require a completed divorce.

The court did not interpret the word “divorce” in the manual’s heading as narrowing the Decree’s scope. The text of the manual imposed no such restriction, and its supporting-document list included a divorce complaint as well as a divorce judgment, anticipating assessment before divorce proceedings had concluded. Making eligibility depend on a final divorce would also conflict with the purpose of allowing continued residence for a foreign spouse whose marriage had broken down primarily because of the Korean spouse’s conduct.

2. Implications

The applicant succeeded in challenging the extension refusal by contesting responsibility for the marital breakdown, even though his argument that normal married life continued was rejected. Facts that did not establish an ongoing marital relationship had a different significance when responsibility was assessed. Child-support payments did not prove that the couple continued married life, but continued support after separation helped rebut an assessment that the applicant bore primary responsibility for the breakdown. In a case where reconciliation is no longer a realistic prospect, relying only on the continued legal existence of the marriage can leave the factual basis for F-6-3 residence unaddressed.

The court treated the requirement that the breakdown arise for reasons not attributable to the applicant as an eligibility question subject to full judicial review, rather than a matter left solely to the authority’s discretion. Although the authority bore the burden of establishing the ground for refusal, the court explained that the applicant also had to actively present and prove facts supporting his position. An applicant can therefore have primary responsibility examined directly in proceedings challenging the extension refusal, without first obtaining a divorce judgment to submit to the immigration authority. In this case, the decision that caused the separation, the reasons for rejecting proposals to resume cohabitation, and continued financial support were examined in those proceedings, and F-6-3 eligibility was established before a divorce became final.
